Apollo 11 was the American spaceflight that first landed humans on the Moon. Commander Neil Armstrong and Lunar Module Pilot Buzz Aldrin landed the Apollo Lunar Module, named Eagle, on July 20, 1969. Armstrong became the first person to step onto the lunar surface six hours and 39 minutes later, famously describing the event as "one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind."

While Armstrong and Aldrin were on the surface, Command Module Pilot Michael Collins flew the Command Module, named Columbia, alone in lunar orbit. Collins was essential to the mission's success, as he had to ensure the spacecraft remained fully operational to bring his crewmates back home. He orbited the Moon 30 times, often completely cut off from radio contact with Earth while passing behind the dark side of the Moon.

The astronauts spent a total of 21 hours and 36 minutes on the lunar surface at a site they named Tranquility Base, before lifting off to rejoin Columbia in lunar orbit. The crew safely returned to Earth and splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24, 1969, fulfilling President John F. Kennedy's national goal proposed in 1961 to land a man on the Moon and return him safely to the Earth before the end of the decade.
